Origins

The Patidar community traces its lineage through the Kshatriya (Ruler/Warrior) varna, specifically the Suryavanshi (Sun Dynasty) line. The broader story begins with the Aryan migration — a movement of peoples westward from Central Asia that eventually reached the Indian subcontinent and gave rise to the Sanskrit-speaking cultures of the Vedic period.

The Kurmis — the farming-warrior group from whom the Patidars descend — were part of the Patidar / Patlik lineage that ultimately settled in North Gujarat, adopting Gujarati as their language and farming as their primary identity.

The journey to Gujarat

  • Before 1500 BCAryans reach the banks of the Amu Darya, north of Afghanistan
  • ~1500 BCAryans arrive on the banks of the Indus River (present-day Pakistan)
  • 1000–1027 ADKurmis move east to the banks of the Ganga
  • 1010–1055 ADKanbis (Kurmis) move to the kingdom of Raja Bhoj in Malwa
  • 1093–1143 ADKanbis invited to Anhilwara (Gujarat) by the Solanki King Siddharaj — they settle in Adalaj, North Gujarat
  • ~1200–1800 ADLeuva Patidars spread through Charotar and South Gujarat
  • ~1830 ADSome Leuvas move to Surat (South Gujarat) via Champaner
  • Early 1900sPatidars begin migrating to British East African colonies

Kadva and Leuva — the two main branches

Kadva Patel

Chandravanshi Kurmi lineage, originally from Karad / Kasur. Spread to Ayodhya, Maharashtra, Andhra, Mysore, Chennai, Kota, Mandsor, Malwa, Siddhpur, Unjha, Mathura, and Saurashtra.

Leuva Patel

Suryavanshi Kurmi lineage, originally from Leava / Lahore. Came to Adalaj in North Gujarat via Uttar Pradesh and Marwar. The Bhakta family belongs to the Bhakta (Ramkabir) sub-group of the Leuva Patidars, whose heartland is the Surat, Navsari, and Raj areas of South Gujarat.

South Gujarat — the heartland

The Leuva Patidar heartland in South Gujarat was originally a single Surat district. As the region's population grew and administration expanded, the district was progressively subdivided:

Surat district (original)
├── Surat — remains
├── Valsad — split from Surat (formerly known as Bulsar under British rule)
├── Tapi — split from Surat
└── Navsari — split from Valsad

This matters for the family tree: the district a village belongs to today may differ from the one it was in when an ancestor was born. The village name itself is the stable identifier across generations — district boundaries changed multiple times during the 20th century.

A voyage to East Africa

The Patidar migration route
South Gujarat → Bombay → Arabian Sea → East Africa → UK / USA / Canada

Beginning in the early 1900s, Patidar families from South Gujarat began migrating to the East African colonies of the British Empire — primarily Kenya, Uganda, Tanzania, Zambia, Malawi, Zimbabwe, Mozambique, and South Africa. They came as farmers and traders, and over the following decades became a significant economic force across the region.

The British Empire's recognition of the Indian community's economic role is visible in the currency and postal history of the era: the East African Currency Board issued notes denominated in Indian monetary units (annas, rupees), and stamps were printed in both English and Gujarati for territories like British East Africa and Zanzibar.

Many Bhakta family members followed this path — a generation born in Gujarat, one or two generations born in East Africa, and subsequent generations born in the UK, USA, Canada, or elsewhere. This is why the family tree asks for both village of origin in Gujarat and country emigrated to: together they let us connect branches that span three or four continents.

Why village of origin matters

Within the Patidar community, common surnames like Patel, Desai, and Amin appear across thousands of unrelated families. A birth year alone narrows very little. But knowing someone was born in Chikhli, Navsari district in 1932 narrows the population to dozens of families at most — villages in South Gujarat were small, close-knit communities where intermarriage was common and records were kept at the village level.

This is why the form asks for village of origin: it is the single strongest identifier for connecting records from different family members who submit separately. The tree covers all 243 Leuva Patidar villages of South Gujarat, with autocomplete to handle the many spelling variations that arose from Gujarati names being romanised by different people over different eras.
